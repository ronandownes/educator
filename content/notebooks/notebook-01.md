# Elicit — four interview uses

## Key Line

**Elicit means deliberately drawing out evidence of what students know, understand or can do before deciding what to do next.**

You could naturally use it in at least four different interview answers:

**Assessment / AfL**

> “I use questioning, mini-whiteboards and retrieval tasks to **elicit evidence of student understanding**, and then I adapt the teaching in response.”

**Prior knowledge / planning**

> “At the beginning of a topic, I **elicit prior knowledge** so I know what is secure, what has been forgotten and what misconceptions I need to address.”

**Class discussion / questioning**

> “Rather than immediately supplying the answer, I use prompts and follow-up questions to **elicit students’ reasoning** and make their mathematical thinking visible.”

**Differentiation / AEN**

> “I vary how I **elicit a response** — orally, visually, through mini-whiteboards, worked examples or practical demonstration — so that difficulty expressing an answer does not hide what a student understands.”

That gives you four useful collocations:

**elicit evidence · elicit prior knowledge · elicit reasoning · elicit a response**

Those are worth putting together as a little concept block.

# “Show me six sentences” is exactly the sort of thing I would add

Rather than permanently storing six sentences for every word, the glossary entry can contain **reusable contexts**.

For **elicit**, clicking **Examples** could show:

- I use retrieval questions to **elicit prior knowledge**.
- Mini-whiteboards allow me to **elicit evidence from the whole class**.
- I use probing questions to **elicit students' reasoning**.
- An exit ticket can **elicit evidence of misconceptions**.
- I vary questioning techniques to **elicit responses from quieter students**.
- Assessment is useful when the evidence I **elicit** changes what I do next.

Now you aren't learning a definition. You're learning **how teachers actually say the word**.

That is much more useful for Monday.
