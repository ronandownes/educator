# CAT4

## Key Line

**CAT4 tells me about how a student reasons — verbally, numerically and visually. It does not tell me the whole student, and I use it alongside classroom evidence, attainment data and professional observation.**

That is the interview line to learn.

## What is CAT4?

**CAT4 = Cognitive Abilities Test, Fourth Edition.**

It is designed to give schools information about a student's **reasoning abilities and likely academic potential**, rather than simply testing what curriculum content they have already learned. It can inform teaching, support, target setting and transition planning. ([GL Support](https://support.gl-education.com/knowledge-base/assessments/cat4-support/general-information/about-cat4?utm_source=chatgpt.com "About CAT4 - Support - GL Education"))

Think:

**CAT4 → How might this learner process information?**

Not:

**CAT4 → What grade will this learner get?**

---

## The Four Batteries

### 🗣️ Verbal Reasoning — WORDS

Ability to reason using **language and verbal concepts**.

Think:

**words → vocabulary → relationships between ideas → language**

A lower verbal score might make me think about:

- vocabulary
- literacy
- understanding complex instructions
- mathematical language
- reading word problems
- processing verbally presented information

A strong verbal student may respond very well to:

- discussion
- questioning
- explanation
- analogy
- verbal reasoning

---

### 🔢 Quantitative Reasoning — NUMBERS

Ability to identify and reason with **numerical relationships and patterns**.

Think:

**number relationships → patterns → mathematical reasoning**

Particularly relevant in Maths.

A weaker quantitative score does **not** mean:

> “This student cannot do Maths.”

It tells me I may need more:

- concrete examples
- representations
- explicit modelling
- worked examples
- pattern recognition
- carefully sequenced steps

---

### 🧩 Non-Verbal Reasoning — SHAPES/PATTERNS

Ability to reason with **visual information without depending heavily on words**.

Think:

**shapes → patterns → relationships → logic**

This can reveal ability that is less obvious from literacy-heavy classroom work.

It can be particularly informative where a student has literacy difficulties or English as an additional language because it places much less reliance on language. ([GL Assessment](https://www.gl-assessment.co.uk/news-hub/blogs/how-cat4-helps-put-all-your-learners-firmly-on-the-map/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "How CAT4 helps put all your learners firmly on the map"))

---

### 🧭 Spatial Ability — MENTAL IMAGES

Ability to **visualise and manipulate shapes mentally**.

Think:

**rotate → fold → transform → visualise**

Relevant to areas such as:

- geometry
- diagrams
- graphs
- transformations
- technical drawing
- STEM
- some aspects of problem solving

GL describes spatial tasks as mentally generating and transforming visual images. ([GL Support](https://support.gl-assessment.co.uk/knowledge-base/assessments/cat4-support/general-information/information-for-parents?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Information for parents - Support - GL Education"))

# The Scores

## SAS — the one to remember

**Standard Age Score.**

This is the number I would look at first.

### **100 = average**

The raw CAT4 result is adjusted for age so the student can be compared fairly with students of approximately the same age. GL describes SAS as the most important CAT4 score for interpretation. ([GL Support](https://support.gl-assessment.co.uk/knowledge-base/assessments/cat4-support/after-the-test/quick-data-guide?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Quick data guide - Support - GL Education"))

Very roughly:

**85 — 115 = broad average range**

About two-thirds of pupils fall within that range on this type of standard-age scale. ([GL Support](https://support.gl-assessment.co.uk/knowledge-base/assessments/vrt-support/administration/understanding-the-vr-scores?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Understanding the VR scores - Support - GL Education"))

So visually:

**↓ 85 ——— 100 ——— 115 ↑**  
below average above

Don't get obsessed with one or two points.

**Look at the profile.**

---

## Percentile

This answers:

> **How did the student compare with others of the same age?**

Example:

**Percentile 80**

means approximately:

> performed as well as or better than **80% of the comparison group**.

It does **not** mean they got 80% of the questions correct.

---

## Stanine

**Stanine = Standard Nine.**

Scores are compressed into **9 bands**.

Easy memory:

**1–3 ↓** below average  
**4–6 →** broadly average  
**7–9 ↑** above average

GL CAT4 reports include SAS, percentile rank and stanine. ([GL Support](https://support.gl-assessment.co.uk/knowledge-base/assessments/cat4-support/after-the-test/understanding-your-data?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Understanding your CAT4 data - Support - GL Education"))

For interview purposes, you do not need to recite a giant conversion table.

# The Important Bit: The Profile

Don't just look at:

> CAT4 mean = 103.

Look across:


| V | Q | NV | S |
| ---------- | --------------- | ------------- | ---------- |
| 🗣️ Verbal | 🔢 Quantitative | 🧩 Non-verbal | 🧭 Spatial |
| 82 | 105 | 119 | 121 |


That profile is **far more interesting**.

Immediately I am thinking:

**Strong visual reasoning + weaker verbal reasoning.**

So I might:

**reduce unnecessary language → model visually → pre-teach vocabulary → use diagrams → check understanding of instructions → allow the student to demonstrate reasoning without everything being dependent on reading.**

That is CAT4 being used professionally.

---

# CAT4 Does NOT Diagnose SEN

This is important.

**CAT4 is not a diagnostic test for dyslexia, ADHD, autism, dyscalculia, etc.**

A pattern might make me think:

> “I need to investigate this further.”

It does **not** allow me to say:

> “The CAT4 proves this student has dyslexia.”

Instead:

**CAT4 + attainment + teacher observation + student voice + previous reports + diagnostic assessment where appropriate.**

---

# CAT4 → Teaching

This is the chain I want you to remember:

### **DATA → BARRIER → STRATEGY**

For example:

**Low verbal reasoning**

→ language may be creating an access barrier

→ vocabulary, visuals, shorter instructions, chunking, modelling, checking understanding.

---

**Strong non-verbal / spatial reasoning**

→ visual reasoning is a strength

→ diagrams, manipulatives, models, GeoGebra, graphical representation.

---

**Low quantitative reasoning**

→ numerical relationships may need more explicit development

→ CRA, worked examples, pattern work, scaffolding, smaller steps.

---

**High scores**

→ potential for greater cognitive challenge

→ rich tasks, extension, deeper questioning, less unnecessary scaffolding.

# CAT4 in a Teacher Interview

If they ask:

**“How would you use CAT4?”**

Your answer can be:

> **I use CAT4 as one part of the learner profile. I look beyond the overall score at verbal, quantitative, non-verbal and spatial reasoning, because differences between those areas can identify both strengths and possible barriers to learning. I then combine that with attainment data, classroom observation and transition information to adapt teaching — for example, using visual representation where spatial reasoning is strong or explicitly scaffolding vocabulary where verbal reasoning is weaker.**

That's enough.

## Burn this into your head

**CAT4 = V Q N S**

🗣️ **V** = Words  
🔢 **Q** = Numbers  
🧩 **N** = Patterns  
🧭 **S** = Visualisation

**100 = average SAS.**

And:

## **CAT4 does not tell me what a child can or cannot achieve. It gives me another lens on how they may learn.**

Yes — **absolutely**.

That is actually one of the **most practical ways** to use CAT4.

If you had a simple **bar chart with V, Q, N, S** on the horizontal axis and the **SAS scores** on the vertical axis, you could very quickly see:

- the **highest area**
- the **lowest area**
- whether the profile is **fairly even**
- whether there is a **spiky profile**
- where there may be **strengths to build from**
- where there may be **barriers to access**

## What you would be looking for

### Even profile

Example:

- V = 98
- Q = 101
- N = 99
- S = 102

That suggests a **fairly balanced profile**.

### Spiky profile

Example:

- V = 82
- Q = 95
- N = 118
- S = 121

That tells you something much more useful:

- **verbal reasoning is comparatively weaker**
- **non-verbal and spatial are strong**

So your teaching response might be:

- reduce language load
- use diagrams
- model visually
- pre-teach vocabulary
- let the student show understanding in more than one way

## Why the bar chart is useful

A bar chart lets you see the profile **at a glance**.

You are not just asking:

> “Is this student average?”

You are asking:

> “How is this student strongest?”
>
> “Where might the barriers be?”
>
> “What kind of teaching might suit them best?”

That is much more useful.

## Important caution

Do **not** over-interpret tiny differences.

For example:

- V = 100
- Q = 103
- N = 98
- S = 101

That is basically a **flat profile**.

But if you had:

- V = 80
- Q = 94
- N = 117
- S = 120

that is a **meaningful pattern**.

So the chart is useful when you look for:

- **clear peaks**
- **clear dips**
- **overall shape**

not just 2 or 3 points difference.

## Best way to think about it

### CAT4 bar chart = profile, not label

You are using it to say:

- **what are this student’s strengths?**
- **what might make learning harder?**
- **how can I adapt access?**

Not:

- “this student is weak”
- “this student cannot do Maths”
- “this student is only this kind of learner”
