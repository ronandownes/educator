## The MECE planning paradigm

### One architecture, seven separate decision domains

I do **not** want a different answer for every possible planning question.

I use one planning architecture and organise the interview territory into seven **mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive** decision domains. Each domain answers a different planning question.

| MECE domain | Core question | What belongs here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. General approach** | What is my overall planning philosophy? | learner, curriculum, evidence, flexibility |
| **2. Curriculum & programme** | What learning is required and by which programme? | specification, learning outcomes, JC, LC, TY, LCA, Level 1/2 |
| **3. Scale & sequence** | Over what time horizon am I planning and in what order? | year, unit, week, lesson, scheme of work, retrieval |
| **4. Learner, access & challenge** | Who is in front of me and what changes because of that? | mixed attainment, AEN, literacy, numeracy, support, challenge |
| **5. Subject pedagogy** | How should this mathematical or scientific idea be taught? | prerequisites, misconceptions, representations, modelling, examples, technology |
| **6. Evidence & adaptation** | How will I know what has been learned and what happens next? | AfL, assessment evidence, re-teaching, pacing, evaluation |
| **7. Collaboration & continuity** | How does my planning connect with other teachers and previous/future learning? | handover, department planning, subject plan, common assessment, cover |

### The important distinction

The **seven domains above are the MECE filing system**.

Once I drill down inside one domain, the subquestions are allowed to be nested. For example, **Year → Unit → Week → Lesson → Adaptation** is a deliberate decomposition of the single domain **Scale & Sequence**. That does not break the MECE principle because those questions are children of one parent category rather than competing top-level categories.

Likewise, the scenario section at the end is **not another MECE layer**. A scenario such as “low-confidence Junior Cycle Maths” simply applies the same seven-domain framework to a particular class.

### Universal execution chain

The MECE map tells me **where a question belongs**. My planning chain tells me **what I actually do**:

**learner / starting point → intended learning → sequence → access & challenge → evidence → adapt → next step**

## What is your general approach to planning and curriculum?

### I start with the learner, align with the curriculum, and plan the evidence I will need.

I begin with the **students in front of me**, establish their real starting point, and identify the intended learning from the relevant **curriculum, specification or programme**.

I then **sequence and map** the learning so that new knowledge builds on secure prerequisite knowledge. I use **UDL, clear modelling, appropriate scaffolding and cognitive-load principles** to design for access and challenge.

Assessment is not an add-on at the end. I plan the **evidence of learning** from the outset and use formative assessment continuously to adjust pace, explanation, support, challenge and the natural next step.

My plan is therefore **clear but flexible**: sufficiently structured to give direction, but responsive enough to change when the evidence from the class says it should.

**learner → curriculum → sequence → access → evidence → adapt**

# Curriculum & Programme

## How do the specification and learning outcomes influence your planning?

### I plan from what students are required to know, understand and do — not from a textbook chapter list.

I go directly to the relevant **curriculum specification, syllabus or programme outcomes** and unpack what students are expected to know, understand and do.

At Junior Cycle I pay particular attention to the **action verbs** — for example *investigate, represent, apply, generalise, interpret, reason* and *solve* — because they indicate the type and depth of learning required.

I then:
- identify prerequisite knowledge;
- connect related outcomes;
- sequence them coherently;
- select suitable learning experiences;
- derive success criteria;
- plan assessment evidence.

The textbook can support that plan, but it does not determine the curriculum.

**specification → unpack → connect → sequence → teach → evidence**

## How do you plan for different programmes and pathways?

### The planning architecture stays consistent; the purpose, outcomes, pacing, context and assessment requirements change.

I begin with the particular programme's **purpose, curriculum requirements and assessment requirements**.

I then adapt:
- the sequence;
- pacing;
- degree of abstraction;
- contexts;
- resources;
- level of scaffolding;
- assessment evidence.

The route is therefore different in Junior Cycle, Senior Cycle, TY, LCA or a learning-support setting, but the fundamental planning process remains the same:

**starting point → intended learning → sequence → support/challenge → evidence → adapt**

## How would your planning differ between Junior Cycle and Senior Cycle Mathematics?

### Junior Cycle emphasises broad mathematical development; Senior Cycle adds stronger level and examination constraints.

At **Junior Cycle**, I plan across the learning outcomes with strong emphasis on:
- mathematical connections;
- investigation;
- reasoning;
- representation;
- problem-solving;
- progression over the three years.

At **Senior Cycle**, I work from the relevant syllabus, level and assessment requirements while maintaining conceptual understanding and problem-solving.

The pace, level of abstraction and assessment demands are different, but both require:
- coherent sequencing;
- retrieval;
- prerequisite knowledge;
- responsive teaching;
- evidence-informed adjustment.

In both cases I plan from the **curriculum and the learners**, not from the textbook alone.

## How would you plan Transition Year Mathematics?

### TY should give students mathematical experiences that an examination-driven year may not.

I would begin by deciding what we want students to gain from TY Mathematics that they may not get from an examination-driven programme.

I would consult the existing school programme, Maths department, TY coordinator and students, and build a flexible programme around:
- mathematical thinking;
- problem-solving;
- financial mathematics;
- data and statistics;
- mathematical modelling;
- coding;
- probability;
- puzzles and strategy;
- measurement;
- real-world projects.

I would use tools such as **Desmos, GeoGebra, spreadsheets or Python** where they genuinely improve the learning.

The emphasis would be on **investigation, application and creation**, rather than simply moving ahead into Leaving Certificate content.

I would still maintain important mathematical fluency through retrieval and regular problem-solving, and review the programme using assessment evidence and student feedback.

## How would your planning differ for LCA Mathematical Applications?

### Relevance and accessibility increase, but the learning remains purposeful and age-appropriate.

I plan directly from the **LCA programme outcomes and assessment requirements**.

I use practical, meaningful contexts such as:
- wages and payslips;
- personal finance;
- budgeting;
- bills;
- measurement;
- travel;
- data;
- everyday problem-solving.

I break learning into achievable stages, revisit essential numeracy and make progress visible.

I also ensure that planning aligns with the relevant **key assignments and programme requirements**.

The aim is not to reduce Mathematics to easy work. It is to make the Mathematics **accessible, useful and connected to students' lives while maintaining worthwhile expectations**.

## How do you plan for students following Level 1 or Level 2 Learning Programmes?

### I begin with priority learning needs and observable progress.

I begin with the student's **priority learning needs**, relevant learning outcomes and existing support planning.

I coordinate with the support team so classroom learning contributes to meaningful targets.

Activities are:
- concrete;
- accessible;
- purposeful;
- connected to communication, numeracy, participation and independence.

I record progress in **small, observable steps** and keep the student included in the life of the class wherever possible.

# Scale & Sequence

## How do you plan for a programme or school year?

### Long-term planning maps the destination, sequence, checkpoints and flexibility across the year.

I begin with:
- the curriculum or programme;
- the class profile;
- handover information;
- previous assessment evidence;
- the point already reached where relevant.

I use early low-stakes diagnostic and retrieval evidence to establish the **real starting point**.

I then map the required learning across the year by:
- identifying major learning outcomes or strands;
- identifying prerequisite relationships;
- sequencing major units;
- estimating time;
- placing retrieval and consolidation points;
- identifying key assessment points;
- allowing contingency for re-teaching, school events and differences in pace.

A yearly plan is therefore a **route map**, not a promise to march through a textbook at a fixed speed.

**starting point → outcomes → map → sequence → checkpoints → adapt**

## How do you plan a unit or topic?

### Unit planning turns a curriculum outcome into a coherent learning journey.

For a unit or topic I ask:

- What should students know, understand and be able to do by the end?
- What prerequisite knowledge does that depend on?
- What misconceptions are likely?
- What representations or examples will make the structure visible?
- What should I model explicitly?
- Where should students investigate, discuss or practise?
- What evidence will show that they are ready to move on?

I then sequence the unit from **foundation to increasing independence and transfer**.

A typical structure might be:

**retrieve prerequisite → introduce/represent → model → guided practice → independent practice → apply → assess → revisit/extend**

## How do you plan for a week of Mathematics lessons?

### A week is a short learning sequence, not five isolated lesson plans.

I begin with where the class currently is and ask:

**What meaningful progress should this class have made by the end of the week?**

I then plan:
- the week's learning goal;
- the order of concepts;
- prerequisite retrieval;
- where explanation and modelling are needed;
- where guided practice becomes independent practice;
- likely misconceptions;
- homework or cumulative practice;
- formative checkpoints.

I deliberately leave flexibility.

If Monday's evidence shows that an essential prerequisite is insecure, Tuesday's plan may need to change. If the class progresses more quickly than expected, I can deepen the work rather than simply adding more routine questions.

**Monday evidence should be allowed to change Friday's plan.**

## How do you plan an individual lesson?

### I plan the learning and evidence first; the activities come afterwards.

For an individual lesson I establish:

**Where are they now?**  
What prior learning needs to be activated?

**Where are they going?**  
What is the precise intended learning?

**How will I get them there?**  
Explanation, modelling, representation, questioning, investigation, guided practice and independent practice as appropriate.

**What barriers or challenge do I anticipate?**  
Vocabulary, misconceptions, processing demands, scaffolds and extension.

**How will I know?**  
Hinge questions, mini-whiteboards, observation, student explanation, work produced or an exit task.

A useful lesson chain is:

**retrieve → clarify goal → teach/model/investigate → guided practice → independent application → check → next step**

## How do you adapt your planning as the week or unit progresses?

### Planning is a feedback loop, not a fixed script.

I compare the intended learning with the evidence students are actually producing.

I may:
- move on;
- slow the pace;
- revisit prerequisite knowledge;
- re-teach using a different representation;
- add guided practice;
- regroup students temporarily;
- increase challenge;
- alter the next lesson;
- move a later topic.

The key principle is that **the next plan should visibly respond to the evidence from the previous learning**.

## How would you develop a scheme of work?

### A scheme of work turns the curriculum into a sequenced, evidence-informed plan over a defined period.

I begin with the relevant specification and unpack the learning outcomes into the knowledge, skills and understanding students need.

I then include:
- learning outcomes;
- prerequisite knowledge;
- sequence and progression;
- approximate timeframe;
- teaching and learning approaches;
- representations and resources;
- retrieval points;
- differentiation and inclusion;
- assessment points;
- consolidation;
- links to later learning.

The scheme remains editable. Timing is indicative and should change when assessment evidence or the class profile requires it.

## How do you balance curriculum coverage with depth of understanding?

### Coverage without learning is not genuine progression.

I prioritise the concepts and skills that **unlock later learning**.

I use formative assessment to judge whether students are secure enough to move on and whether a short revisit of prerequisite knowledge is necessary.

I use:
- retrieval;
- cumulative review;
- interleaving;
- targeted re-teaching

so that earlier learning can reappear without repeatedly stopping the entire programme.

I maintain purposeful pace, but not at the cost of foundational understanding.

## How do you build retrieval, consolidation and revision into long-term planning?

### Remembering should be designed into the scheme, not added at the end.

I deliberately schedule previously learned material to reappear through:
- retrieval starters;
- homework;
- questioning;
- cumulative review;
- interleaving;
- low-stakes quizzes;
- consolidation lessons.

I place consolidation points after significant units and before new learning that depends on them.

Revision therefore becomes **continuous strengthening of memory and connections**, rather than emergency re-teaching before an examination.

# Learner, Access & Challenge

## How do you establish the real starting point for a class?

### I triangulate several sources rather than relying on one score.

I use:
- handover information;
- previous work;
- assessment evidence;
- learner profiles;
- Student Support Files where relevant;
- short retrieval tasks;
- classroom observation;
- student explanation.

I want to know:
- what students can already do;
- what is insecure;
- likely misconceptions;
- confidence;
- access barriers;
- relevant strengths and interests.

That gives me a defensible **starting point** for planning.

## How do you plan for a mixed-attainment class?

### Common worthwhile learning; different routes, supports and depths.

I begin with a common, worthwhile learning goal and plan multiple routes into the same learning.

Support may include:
- clear modelling;
- worked examples;
- chunked tasks;
- manipulatives;
- visual representations;
- vocabulary support;
- guided practice;
- prompts.

Challenge may include:
- justification;
- generalisation;
- comparing methods;
- unfamiliar applications;
- proof or reasoning;
- creating examples or counterexamples.

Formative assessment helps me vary the amount of support and challenge during the lesson.

The aim is **different levels of assistance without different expectations of participation**.

## How do you provide both support and appropriate challenge?

### I vary support and depth rather than simply giving easier or more work.

For students requiring support I may use:
- modelling;
- worked examples;
- prompts;
- concrete or visual representation;
- key vocabulary;
- additional guided practice;
- reduced organisational load.

For students requiring greater challenge I increase the **cognitive demand** through:
- reasoning;
- justification;
- generalisation;
- method comparison;
- unfamiliar problem-solving;
- transfer.

Challenge is not “finish ten more questions”.

## How do you plan for a student with dyslexia?

### Remove unnecessary literacy barriers without lowering the mathematical or scientific demand.

I first establish the student's individual profile because dyslexia does not present identically in every learner.

In planning I may use:
- explicit teaching of key vocabulary;
- uncluttered resources;
- short, clear written instructions;
- verbal explanation alongside written information;
- diagrams and visual representation;
- worked examples;
- manageable text density;
- additional processing time where appropriate;
- assistive technology where it removes a genuine barrier.

I distinguish between **difficulty accessing the wording of a task** and **difficulty understanding the underlying concept**.

The objective is access and increasing independence, not permanent over-scaffolding.

## How do you plan for a student with dyscalculia or persistent numeracy difficulty?

### Identify the precise numerical barrier and rebuild meaning, not just procedures.

I would establish whether the difficulty concerns:
- number sense;
- place value;
- magnitude;
- basic facts;
- fractions;
- proportional reasoning;
- notation;
- sequencing;
- working memory demands.

I would use:
- concrete materials;
- visual models;
- number lines;
- consistent representations;
- explicit modelling;
- small steps;
- cumulative retrieval;
- opportunities to verbalise reasoning.

I would connect symbols to meaning and avoid treating repeated incorrect answers as simply a need for more of the same worksheet.

## How do you plan for a high-achieving autistic student?

### Autism does not imply low attainment; the student may need structure and very substantial intellectual challenge.

I begin with the individual learner profile rather than assuming a standard autistic presentation.

A high-achieving student may have:
- exceptional subject knowledge;
- strong pattern recognition;
- deep special interests;
- sophisticated reasoning;
- preference for precision and predictability;
- difficulty with ambiguity, transitions or some collaborative situations.

I would maintain genuine academic challenge through:
- deeper reasoning;
- generalisation;
- proof;
- mathematical modelling;
- open problems;
- independent investigation;
- sophisticated extension connected to the core learning.

At the same time I may provide:
- clear expectations;
- explicit task structure;
- advance notice of changes;
- precise language;
- predictable routines.

Where appropriate, a special interest can become a route into learning, but I would not allow it to narrow the student's curriculum unnecessarily.

## How do you plan for an autistic student with strong ability but organisational or executive-function difficulties?

### Separate cognitive ability from organisation and task management.

A student may understand the Mathematics very well while struggling to:
- start;
- organise materials;
- sequence steps;
- shift between tasks;
- manage time;
- record homework;
- complete extended work.

I would reduce unnecessary executive load through:
- visible lesson structure;
- clear starting points;
- chunked tasks;
- short checklists;
- explicit transitions;
- one instruction at a time where useful;
- interim deadlines;
- consistent routines;
- models of what completed work looks like.

I would check understanding separately from completion.

The long-term aim is:

**external structure → shared structure → increasing self-management**

rather than permanent dependence on adult prompting.

## How do you plan for a student with ADHD or significant attention/executive-function needs?

### Structure attention and task initiation while preserving worthwhile learning.

I would plan:
- clear goals;
- short, purposeful task phases;
- active participation;
- explicit transitions;
- visible progress;
- reduced unnecessary copying;
- frequent checks for understanding;
- opportunities for appropriate movement where possible;
- supports for task initiation and completion.

I would distinguish between:
- not attending;
- not understanding;
- forgetting instructions;
- becoming overloaded;
- needing a more active route into the task.

The response should target the actual barrier rather than simply increasing sanctions or repeating instructions.

## How do you plan for a student with emotional needs or anxiety?

### Predictability, relationship and emotional safety may be prerequisites for access to learning.

I would use the student support information and collaborate with relevant staff to understand:
- known triggers;
- helpful strategies;
- signs of dysregulation;
- agreed supports.

My planning may include:
- predictable routines;
- clear advance expectations;
- an achievable entry task;
- reduced unnecessary public performance;
- manageable transitions;
- calm opportunities to re-enter learning after difficulty;
- visible success criteria;
- appropriate choice.

I maintain the learning goal, but I recognise that a student who is highly dysregulated may first need support to become available for learning.

## How do you plan for a student with behavioural needs?

### Good planning reduces avoidable triggers while keeping expectations clear and consistent.

I distinguish behavioural need from academic ability.

I consider:
- when difficulties tend to occur;
- task difficulty;
- transitions;
- peer dynamics;
- unclear instructions;
- excessive waiting time;
- attention-seeking patterns;
- existing support plans.

I plan:
- explicit expectations;
- predictable routines;
- purposeful pace;
- accessible task entry;
- active participation;
- clear transitions;
- positive reinforcement;
- proportionate responses;
- opportunities to repair and re-engage.

Behaviour is useful information about the learning environment or the student's needs, but it does not remove expectations.

## How do you plan for literacy difficulties in Mathematics or Science?

### Teach the literacy of the subject explicitly.

I identify the language that may block access:
- command words;
- subject vocabulary;
- dense word problems;
- comparative language;
- technical definitions;
- extended written explanations.

I plan:
- explicit vocabulary instruction;
- visual support;
- sentence stems where useful;
- modelling of how to read a problem;
- oral rehearsal before written response;
- worked examples of mathematical or scientific communication.

I want students to gain access to the subject **without the literacy scaffold becoming permanent**.

## How do you plan for a learning-support Science top-up class where students are coping in mainstream Science?

### Use the extra time to strengthen access and independence, not simply repeat the mainstream lesson.

I would liaise with the Science teacher and review current evidence.

If the students are broadly coping with ordinary classwork, I would avoid unnecessary remediation.

Instead, I could use their interest in Science to strengthen:
- scientific vocabulary;
- literacy;
- numeracy;
- graph interpretation;
- data handling;
- measurement;
- experimental reasoning;
- written explanation;
- independent learning.

For example, students might interpret real experimental data, graph results or explain why an investigation did not produce the expected pattern.

The student's **interest in Science becomes a vehicle for strengthening wider learning skills**.

# Subject Pedagogy

## How do prior knowledge and likely misconceptions influence your planning?

### I identify what the new learning depends on before I choose the activity.

Before teaching a topic I identify:
- prerequisite knowledge;
- likely misconceptions;
- common procedural errors;
- vocabulary;
- representations students should already recognise.

Retrieval and diagnostic questioning tell me whether those foundations are secure.

I then choose examples and representations that make the structure visible and deliberately expose misconceptions.

If the evidence shows a gap, I revisit the prerequisite or change the representation before building further.

## How do you decide which representations, examples and resources to use?

### I choose the representation that reveals the mathematical structure most clearly.

I ask:
- What is difficult about this idea?
- Which representation makes that relationship visible?
- What cognitive load does the resource add?
- Can students connect the representation to symbols and language?
- Does the example vary one feature at a time so students see what matters?

I may move through:
- concrete materials;
- diagrams;
- graphs;
- tables;
- verbal reasoning;
- symbolic notation.

The aim is not to use multiple representations for decoration. It is to help students **connect representations and eventually reason flexibly between them**.

## How do you decide what to model explicitly and what students should discover or investigate?

### I use explicit teaching when guidance is needed and investigation when exploration has a clear mathematical purpose.

I model explicitly when students need:
- a new procedure;
- a worked example;
- a way of organising thinking;
- a precise convention;
- a reduction in unnecessary cognitive load.

I use investigation when students can productively:
- notice a pattern;
- make a conjecture;
- compare cases;
- test an idea;
- reason from evidence.

The choice depends on the learning goal and students' prior knowledge.

I would not use discovery simply because it appears more active, and I would not model so much that students never need to think independently.

## How do you plan examples and questions?

### Examples and questions should expose structure and generate evidence.

I select examples deliberately rather than randomly.

I may:
- vary one feature while holding others constant;
- include a boundary case;
- include a non-example;
- compare two methods;
- move from routine to unfamiliar;
- ask students to predict before calculating.

Questions serve different purposes:
- retrieval;
- checking understanding;
- exposing misconceptions;
- prompting explanation;
- deepening reasoning;
- deciding whether to move on.

## How do you decide when technology such as GeoGebra, Desmos or Python adds value?

### Technology should reveal, test or automate something that improves the learning.

I use digital tools when they improve:
- visualisation;
- dynamic representation;
- rapid variation;
- data analysis;
- simulation;
- accessibility;
- feedback.

For example, students can vary coefficients dynamically in Desmos or GeoGebra and form a conjecture about what changes.

But the technology is not the endpoint. I still ask students to:
- articulate the pattern;
- explain it;
- justify it;
- connect it to formal Mathematics.

## How do you plan purposeful practice?

### Practice should move from security to discrimination and transfer.

I plan:
- guided practice after modelling;
- sufficient independent practice for fluency;
- cumulative retrieval;
- mixed practice where students must choose a method;
- unfamiliar applications;
- opportunities to explain reasoning.

The aim is not simply volume.

Students need eventually to recognise **which Mathematics to use and why**, not just execute a method after being told which chapter they are in.

# Evidence & Adaptation

## How do you incorporate formative assessment into your planning?

### I plan the evidence I need before I plan all the activities.

I identify where in the lesson or sequence I may need to make a decision.

I then use appropriate evidence such as:
- effective questioning;
- hinge questions;
- mini-whiteboards;
- observation;
- student explanation;
- short retrieval;
- exit tasks.

These checks are connected to:
- intended learning;
- likely misconceptions;
- success criteria.

I use the response to adjust:
- pace;
- explanation;
- grouping;
- guided practice;
- support;
- challenge;
- the natural next step.

## How do you use assessment evidence to inform future planning?

### I look beyond the mark to identify patterns in learning.

I analyse:
- what students understood;
- where strategies broke down;
- recurring misconceptions;
- whether difficulty is conceptual, procedural or interpretive;
- whether a problem affects the whole class or a smaller group.

I then decide whether to:
- move on;
- re-teach;
- change representation;
- provide targeted support;
- use flexible grouping;
- increase challenge;
- revisit a prerequisite.

The next plan should **visibly respond to what the evidence revealed**.

## How do you adapt when students have not understood a topic?

### Diagnose before re-teaching.

I first identify the precise misconception or missing prerequisite.

I do not simply repeat the same lesson more slowly.

I may change:
- the representation;
- example;
- explanation;
- task;
- level of guidance.

I then rebuild understanding through guided practice and check again using a focused hinge question, mini-whiteboards or exit task.

Once the evidence is secure enough, I reconnect the learning to the wider sequence.

## How do you use student data without allowing it to limit expectations?

### Data informs the starting point; it does not define the ceiling.

I use prior attainment and assessment data to identify:
- patterns;
- strengths;
- likely gaps;
- students who may need particular support.

But I triangulate it with current classroom evidence.

I avoid turning a historical score into a fixed judgement about what a student can achieve.

Planning should respond to **current evidence and progress**, while maintaining appropriately ambitious expectations.

## How do you evaluate and improve future planning?

### I compare intended learning with actual learning.

At the end of a unit, term or year I review:
- what was intended;
- what was actually taught;
- what students actually learned;
- pacing;
- misconceptions;
- resources;
- representations;
- assessment evidence;
- support and challenge.

I record what should be:
- retained;
- moved;
- removed;
- strengthened;
- re-sequenced.

That reflection becomes evidence for the next plan rather than relying on memory.

# Collaboration & Continuity

## What is the difference between a subject plan and a scheme of work?

### The subject plan is the department framework; the scheme of work maps learning for a particular cohort or period.

The **subject plan** addresses broader departmental organisation such as:
- curriculum provision;
- assessment;
- inclusion;
- resources;
- common expectations;
- departmental procedures.

A **scheme of work** is more specific. It sequences learning for a particular year group or class across a defined period.

My individual planning sits within both while remaining responsive to the students in front of me.

## How do you contribute to collaborative planning and consistency within the Maths department?

### Consistency means common entitlement, not identical teaching.

I contribute by sharing:
- resources;
- assessment evidence;
- common misconceptions;
- successful approaches;
- curriculum interpretation.

As a department we may agree:
- essential learning outcomes;
- broad sequencing;
- common terminology;
- assessment points;
- expectations.

Teachers still retain professional judgement about how best to teach the actual class in front of them.

I record useful decisions so collaboration changes practice rather than remaining informal conversation.

## How do you ensure continuity and progression from one year to the next?

### Start from what was learned, not merely what was covered.

I use:
- agreed schemes of work;
- assessment records;
- handover information;
- learner profiles.

I identify what has been taught and how securely it has been learned.

I then map new learning onto the prerequisite knowledge students should possess.

Early retrieval confirms the starting point and exposes gaps.

Where necessary I revisit essential foundations while maintaining forward progression.

## How do you use handover information when taking over a new class?

### Handover is a starting hypothesis, not a fixed judgement.

I review:
- the point reached in the scheme;
- recent assessment evidence;
- learner profile;
- AEN/support information;
- strengths;
- misconceptions;
- strategies that have worked.

Where possible I speak with the previous teacher.

I then use early retrieval and classroom observation to confirm or revise that picture.

## How do you plan when taking over a class part-way through the year?

### Preserve continuity first, then establish your own evidence.

I begin with:
- handover;
- scheme of work;
- work already completed;
- recent assessment evidence;
- learner profile.

I establish what the class is expected to know and use short diagnostic retrieval to determine what is actually secure.

I then continue the existing sequence where appropriate, while adjusting:
- pacing;
- re-teaching;
- supports;
- assessment;
- next steps.

I do not restart the course simply because I am the new teacher.

## How do you plan one class when you are covering for another teacher for the day?

### For one period of cover, I preserve continuity rather than redesigning the course.

I do a **rapid profile study**.

Before the class, where the information is available, I check:
- class and level;
- point in the scheme;
- regular teacher's instructions;
- relevant learner/AEN alerts;
- available resources.

I identify one achievable lesson goal and the prerequisite knowledge required.

At the start of the lesson I use quick retrieval or questioning to establish where students are.

I then teach the planned learning, make reasonable adjustments for the learners in front of me, finish with a quick check, and leave concise feedback for the regular teacher.

**profile → teacher's plan / SoW → lesson goal → quick retrieval → teach → check → report back**

The distinction is important:

**one-day cover = preserve continuity**

**mid-year takeover = establish continuity, then assume responsibility for future planning**

## How do common assessments influence planning?

### Common assessment is useful when it is aligned and the evidence is used.

Common assessments can help a department:
- establish shared expectations;
- compare patterns in learning;
- identify common misconceptions;
- review sequencing.

They are only useful if they assess learning students have actually had an opportunity to develop.

The purpose is not simply to generate comparable marks. The evidence should inform subsequent planning.

## How do you plan collaboratively while retaining professional autonomy?

### Agree the entitlement and destination; use professional judgement about the route.

I collaborate on:
- curriculum interpretation;
- broad sequencing;
- assessment expectations;
- common terminology;
- key resources where useful.

Within that framework I adapt:
- explanation;
- representation;
- pacing;
- grouping;
- scaffolding;
- extension

to the students actually in front of me.

# Scenario Breakouts

### These are applications of the MECE framework, not additional planning philosophies.

For any scenario I quickly run the same diagnostic:

**Who are the learners? → What is the intended learning? → What scale am I planning at? → What subject-pedagogy decisions matter? → What access/challenge is needed? → What evidence will I gather? → Who do I need to coordinate with?**

## Low-confidence Junior Cycle Mathematics class

### Confidence is built through successful participation in worthwhile Mathematics.

I establish whether the difficulty is primarily:
- conceptual;
- procedural;
- literacy-related;
- confidence-related;
- missing prerequisite knowledge.

I use low-stakes evidence rather than beginning with another high-stakes experience.

My planning emphasises:
- achievable but worthwhile goals;
- clear modelling;
- worked examples;
- multiple representations;
- manageable steps;
- guided practice;
- frequent retrieval of accessible prior learning;
- regular formative checks;
- visible progress.

If students struggle, I identify the precise barrier and change the representation, explanation or level of guidance.

I do not equate low confidence with low potential.

## Strong or high-achieving Junior Cycle Mathematics class

### Increase depth, not merely speed or volume.

I would not simply race through the course.

I increase challenge through:
- reasoning;
- justification;
- generalisation;
- proof;
- non-routine problems;
- unfamiliar contexts;
- conjecture;
- comparison of methods.

Technology can support exploration, but students still need to articulate and justify the Mathematics.

## Leaving Certificate Ordinary Level Mathematics

### Build dependable understanding, methods and examination independence.

I plan around:
- syllabus requirements;
- prerequisite knowledge;
- conceptual clarity;
- common misconceptions;
- cumulative retrieval;
- examination demands.

I model not only how to perform a method, but how to:
- interpret the question;
- identify relevant Mathematics;
- structure a solution;
- check whether an answer makes sense.

As the examination approaches I progressively reduce scaffolding and increase integrated examination-style practice.

## Results-focused Higher Level Leaving Certificate Mathematics

### Secure understanding must transfer into method selection and performance under examination conditions.

I work backwards from:
- syllabus;
- assessment demands;
- time available;
- prerequisite chains.

I plan:
- coherent topic sequencing;
- cumulative retrieval;
- interleaving;
- sophisticated worked examples;
- unfamiliar problems;
- examination-style questions;
- timed practice;
- error analysis;
- targeted re-teaching.

I distinguish between:

**not knowing the Mathematics**

and

**knowing it but failing to recognise when or how to deploy it under examination conditions**.

My examining experience helps me understand national marking standards and clear mathematical communication, but classroom learning remains broader than mark acquisition.

## TY Mathematics project

### TY gives space for investigation, modelling and application.

A unit might begin with a genuine problem such as:

**What does it really cost to run a car for one year?**

Students could:
- gather real data;
- make assumptions;
- model costs in a spreadsheet;
- test scenarios;
- compare outcomes;
- justify conclusions.

That integrates:
- percentages;
- estimation;
- modelling;
- data;
- digital literacy;
- communication.

The Mathematics is being used to investigate a real question rather than presented as isolated exercises.

## TY Physics teaser lesson

### Begin with a phenomenon worth explaining.

I would avoid starting with a page of formulae.

I might ask:

**Can we estimate (g) using a phone and a falling object?**

or

**Can we predict where a projectile will land before we release it?**

Students:
- predict;
- observe;
- measure;
- model;
- test;
- explain;
- refine.

I introduce the formal Physics when it helps explain something students have already encountered.

The desired outcome is curiosity together with a genuine taste of physical reasoning.

## Junior Cycle Science class with weak numeracy

### Teach the Mathematics embedded in the Science explicitly.

I identify numeracy demands such as:
- graph scales;
- unit conversion;
- ratio;
- percentage;
- proportionality;
- formula substitution;
- measurement.

I model these within the scientific context rather than assuming students will automatically transfer learning from Mathematics.

## Practical Science class where engagement is high but theory is weak

### Use the practical experience as the anchor for abstraction.

I move through:

**observe → record → identify pattern → explain → model → predict**

The experiment is not a break from theory. It generates evidence from which the theory can be built.

## Applied Mathematics

### Make the modelling cycle explicit.

I ask students to move through:

**physical situation → assumptions → diagram → mathematical model → solve → interpret → evaluate**

I make assumptions visible and ask what has been ignored or simplified.

A mathematically correct result is incomplete if the student cannot interpret what it means physically.

## Leaving Certificate Computer Science or Coding

### Move students from following code to independent computational thinking.

I plan progression through:
- decomposition;
- prediction;
- tracing;
- worked examples;
- partially completed code;
- debugging;
- modification;
- independent design.

The progression is:

**follow → explain → modify → design**

A program that runs is not enough if the student cannot explain why it works.

# Final Recall

### Seven doors; one planning architecture.

**General approach** — What is my planning philosophy?  
**Curriculum & programme** — What learning is required?  
**Scale & sequence** — Over what time and in what order?  
**Learner, access & challenge** — Who is in front of me?  
**Subject pedagogy** — How should this idea be taught?  
**Evidence & adaptation** — How will I know and respond?  
**Collaboration & continuity** — How does this connect with others and what came before?

Then, for any scenario:

**starting point → intended learning → sequence → access/challenge → evidence → adapt → next step**
