## How do you assess learning?

I use a range of formative and summative assessment: **questioning ❓, observation 👁️, mini-whiteboards 🧾, discussion 🗣️, written work ✍️, retrieval quizzes 🔄, topic tests 📝 and formal assessments 📊**.

I assess the **quality of students’ thinking, not just the final answer**. I look at how students **represent 🔷, explain 💬 and justify 🧠** their thinking, and use that evidence to identify **strengths, misconceptions and depth of understanding**. That then informs the next teaching move: **extend 🧗, practise ✏️, reteach ↩️, change representation 🔁, adjust pace ⏱️, regroup 👥 or provide targeted support 🤲**.

I also use digital tools 💻 where they add value. Immediate feedback ⚡ can support **assessment as learning**, because students can recognise errors 👀, adjust their thinking 🔧 and try again independently 🔄 rather than waiting for me to tell them whether they are right.

**evidence 📊 → interpret 🔎 → diagnose 🩺 → respond 🔧 → monitor 👁️**

📊 → 🔎 → 🩺 → 🔧 → 👁️

## How do you establish a real starting point?

### I triangulate evidence rather than relying on one mark or report.

I use transition and handover information, previous reports, prior assessment evidence, short retrieval or diagnostic tasks, questioning, observation and the student's explanation of their thinking.

I do not assume that a previous mark tells me exactly what a student can do. Early diagnostic assessment helps me establish what the student knows, what they misunderstand and what may be preventing them from showing what they know.

**handover → retrieve → question → observe → diagnose**

## How do you use a hinge question to expose a misconception?

### A useful hinge question is quick, discriminating and built around plausible wrong answers.

For example, when solving \(3x+5=17\), answers such as \(x=6\), \(x=\frac{22}{3}\) or \(x=12\) point toward different errors. I design distractors around likely misconceptions, so the answer shows not simply whether the student is wrong but how they may be thinking.

**question → response → misconception → teaching move → recheck**

## How do you distinguish an access barrier from a misconception?

### I change the access route while keeping the learning demand constant.

I might read a question aloud, simplify unnecessary language, highlight key information, add a diagram or ask the student to explain the mathematics orally. If the student can then complete the mathematics, the original difficulty may have been literacy or access. If the mathematical error remains, I investigate the conceptual misconception.

**Remove the possible barrier without removing the learning.**

## What do you do after a mark is recorded?

### The mark is the start of the response, not the end of assessment.

I ask what the evidence shows, whether the pattern is class-wide or individual, who needs reteaching, scaffolding or extension, what should change in the next lesson, how students will act on feedback and how I will check that the gap has closed.

**evidence → diagnosis → response → recheck**



## What is your understanding of Assessment as Learning?

### Assessment as Learning teaches students to judge the quality of their own learning and decide what to do next.

Students use **clear success criteria, exemplars, guided self-assessment, peer assessment, retrieval and immediate feedback** to compare their work with the expected standard, recognise strengths and errors, and make improvements.

Digital tools can support this by allowing students to receive immediate feedback, adjust their response and try again independently.

The aim is to develop **metacognition, ownership and self-regulation**, so that students become less dependent on teacher correction.

**criteria → attempt → compare → reflect → adjust**

🎯 → ✍️ → 👀 → 🪞 → 🔧

## Assessment for Learning (AfL)?

### Assessment for Learning makes the learner's current understanding visible while there is still time to improve it.

AfL is the deliberate use of evidence during learning to identify where students are, where they need to go and what will help them get there. I use learning intentions, success criteria, questioning, retrieval, mini-whiteboards, observation and feedback to make understanding visible.

The important point is that I act on the evidence. I may adapt the explanation, revisit prior knowledge, change representation, provide additional practice or increase challenge.

**clarify → elicit → interpret → feedback → adapt**

🎯 → 💬 → 🔎 → ↩️ → 🔧

## How do you know students are actually learning?

### Performance today is not enough; I look for understanding that can be retrieved, explained and transferred later.

I check learning in different forms and at different times. Students should be able to recall important knowledge, explain their reasoning, represent ideas in more than one way and apply learning when the surface features of a question change.

I therefore use retrieval over time, questioning, varied examples and cumulative assessment rather than relying only on completion or immediate correct answers.

**retrieve → explain → represent → transfer → retain**

🔄 → 🗣️ → 🧩 → 🔀 → 🔒

## How do you use assessment evidence to inform your planning?

### Planning is provisional until assessment evidence tells me what the class actually needs.

I use assessment evidence to identify strengths, gaps, misconceptions and patterns across the class and for individual students. That informs the pace, sequencing, grouping, scaffolding, level of challenge and amount of retrieval or consolidation I build into subsequent lessons.

I also use it longitudinally. If evidence shows weak retention, I revisit learning; if understanding is secure, I move forward or deepen the level of challenge.

**assess → identify → prioritise → plan → revisit**

📊 → 🔎 → ⭐ → 🧭 → ↩️

## How do you evaluate your own teaching as distinct from students' learning?

### I judge my teaching by the learning it produces, not by how smoothly the lesson appeared to run.

After a lesson I consider whether the intended learning was achieved, which explanations or representations worked, where misconceptions persisted and which students were not yet accessing the learning.

I use student responses, work, questioning and exit evidence to decide whether the issue was prior knowledge, task design, explanation, pacing, challenge or support, and I adjust the next lesson accordingly.

**intention → evidence → reflect → identify → adjust**

🎯 → 📊 → 🪞 → 🔎 → 🔧

## How do you use questioning as assessment rather than simply checking answers?

### Good questioning exposes thinking; it does not merely collect answers.

I plan questions that move from recall into explanation, comparison, justification and application. I use wait time, cold or targeted questioning appropriately, mini-whiteboards and follow-up prompts such as “why?”, “how do you know?” and “can you represent that another way?”.

I listen for misconceptions and the quality of reasoning, then decide whether to probe further, scaffold, reteach or extend.

**ask → wait → probe → interpret → respond**

❓ → ⏳ → 🔍 → 🧠 → 🔧

## How do you use observation and student work as assessment evidence?

### What students say, write, draw and do all provide evidence of understanding.

As students work, I circulate and look for strategy choice, representations, errors, independence, fluency and misconceptions. Written work lets me see patterns over time and distinguish a one-off slip from a conceptual difficulty.

I use those observations to give immediate feedback, select examples for whole-class discussion and identify students who need additional support or greater challenge.

**observe → notice → interpret → feedback → target**

👁️ → 📌 → 🔎 → 💬 → 🎯

## How do you assess students with additional educational needs while maintaining appropriate expectations?

### I remove barriers to demonstrating learning without removing the learning itself.

I begin from the intended learning outcome and consider what access supports are needed: clearer language, chunking, visual or concrete representations, additional processing time, assistive technology or alternative ways of responding where appropriate.

I assess progress from the student's starting point while maintaining ambitious, realistic expectations and using evidence to decide the next achievable step.

**outcome → access → evidence → progress → challenge**

🎯 → 🚪 → 📊 → ➡️ → 🧗

## How do you use assessment data without labelling or limiting students?

### Assessment data is a starting point for professional judgement, not a ceiling on a student.

I use scores and other assessment information alongside classroom evidence, student work, observation and knowledge of the learner. I look for patterns and areas requiring support rather than treating a single result as a fixed measure of ability.

I keep expectations high, monitor progress and remain open to evidence that a student is ready for more challenge or needs a different approach.

**data → context → judgement → support → review**

📈 → 🧩 → ⚖️ → 🤲 → 🔎

## How do you know whether an intervention or additional support is working?

### An intervention is effective only if the evidence shows meaningful progress toward a defined goal.

I establish a clear baseline and a specific target, identify what evidence will indicate improvement and review progress after an appropriate period. I compare the student's current performance with the starting point and also consider whether improvement is transferring into normal classroom learning.

If progress is limited, I review the strategy, intensity, duration or underlying need with the relevant colleagues and adjust the support.

**baseline → target → intervene → measure → review**

📍 → 🎯 → 🤲 → 📊 → 🔎

## How do formative and summative assessment work together?

### Summative assessment tells me where learning has reached; formative assessment helps me decide how to move it forward.

Formative assessment is continuous and responsive: questioning, observation, retrieval, discussion and feedback shape teaching while learning is happening. Summative assessment provides a more formal snapshot at a point in time through tests, examinations or completed assessment tasks.

I use summative outcomes formatively as well, analysing patterns and misconceptions so that the result informs revision, planning and future teaching.

**formative → respond → summative → analyse → feed forward**

🔄 → 🔧 → 📝 → 🔎 → ➡️

## What role do common assessments, CBAs and formal examinations play in your assessment approach?

### Formal assessment gives valuable evidence, but it sits within a much wider picture of student learning.

Common assessments can support consistency across classes and help departments identify shared strengths or gaps. CBAs provide opportunities to assess broader skills and learning in the context intended by the curriculum, while formal examinations give structured evidence of attainment under common conditions.

I use all of these alongside day-to-day formative evidence and analyse outcomes to inform teaching rather than allowing any single assessment to define the learner.

**common → CBA → exam → analyse → inform**

🤝 → 🗂️ → 📝 → 🔎 → 🧭

## What makes feedback useful?

### Feedback is useful when the student knows what is working, what needs improvement and what to do next.

Depending on the task, that may be verbal feedback in the moment, whole-class feedback, a worked example, comment-only feedback or a short next-step target.

The important part is that feedback is specific, manageable and actionable, and that students have an opportunity to use it rather than simply receive it.

**notice → specify → next step → act → improve**

👀 → 💬 → ➡️ → ✏️ → 📈

## How do you give effective feedback without over-marking?

### I mark for impact, not for volume.

I focus feedback on the learning intention and the most important next step rather than correcting everything on the page. I use live verbal feedback, whole-class feedback for common misconceptions, selective written comments, codes or exemplars where appropriate.

I then build time into the lesson for students to respond, correct or improve their work, because that is where the feedback produces learning.

**focus → select → feedback → respond → improve**

🎯 → ⭐ → 💬 → ✏️ → 📈

## When would you use verbal, written or whole-class feedback?

### I choose the feedback method that gives the clearest next step with the least unnecessary delay.

I use verbal feedback when an immediate prompt can move learning forward during the task. Written feedback is useful when the student needs a durable, specific next step or when I want them to revisit work independently. Whole-class feedback is efficient when several students share the same misconception or improvement point.

The format changes; the purpose remains the same: make the next step clear and actionable.

**immediate → individual → pattern → next step → action**

⚡ → 👤 → 👥 → ➡️ → ✏️

## How do you get students to act on feedback rather than simply read it?

### Feedback becomes learning only when students do something with it.

I build response time into lessons. Students may correct an error, improve an explanation, complete a similar problem, redraft part of a response or explain what they changed and why.

I then check the response so that feedback becomes a short cycle of evidence, action and reassessment rather than a comment at the end of a piece of work.

**feedback → act → practise → recheck → improve**

💬 → ✏️ → 🔄 → 👀 → 📈

## How do self-assessment and peer assessment fit into your practice?

### Self and peer assessment help students internalise the criteria for quality.

I use clear success criteria, exemplars and structured prompts so students can identify strengths and next steps in their own or another student's work. The process needs modelling and a respectful classroom culture; it is not simply asking students to give each other marks.

Used well, it develops metacognition, ownership and the ability to recognise quality independently.

**criteria → model → compare → reflect → improve**

📋 → 👨‍🏫 → ⚖️ → 🪞 → 📈

## Share an effective method you have used to assign and assess classwork or homework.

### I prefer short, purposeful tasks that give me usable evidence rather than work completed for its own sake.

For example, I may set a small set of carefully chosen mathematics questions that include retrieval, a current learning focus and one transfer or challenge item. At the next lesson I sample responses, use mini-whiteboards or discussion to expose methods and address common misconceptions efficiently.

That makes the task part of the learning sequence: practise, retrieve, assess and respond.

**assign → retrieve → sample → diagnose → respond**

📝 → 🔄 → 👀 → 🔎 → 🔧

## How do you report progress?

### Good reporting combines attainment, progress and the next learning priority.

I draw on a range of evidence rather than a mark alone: formal assessments, classwork, homework, questioning, engagement with learning and progress over time. I report accurately and proportionately within the school's agreed systems.

Where possible, I make the next step clear so that reporting is informative rather than merely descriptive.

**attainment → progress → evidence → priority → report**

📊 → 📈 → 🗂️ → 🎯 → 🗣️

## How do you report progress to parents or guardians?

### Parents should leave with a clear picture of progress, evidence and the most useful next step.

I communicate strengths first, then give an accurate account of attainment and progress using specific evidence. If there is a concern, I describe it clearly and professionally without labelling the student, explain what support is already in place and identify a practical next step.

I also listen to the parent or guardian because information from home may help us understand the student's learning more fully.

**strength → evidence → concern → action → partnership**

⭐ → 📊 → ⚠️ → 🔧 → 🤝

## How would you prepare for a parent-teacher meeting?

### I prepare evidence, a clear message and one useful next step for each student.

Before the meeting I review recent assessment evidence, classwork, engagement, progress over time and any relevant support information. I identify a genuine strength, the main learning priority and a practical action that would help.

I keep the conversation concise, specific and respectful, record anything significant as required and follow up where necessary.

**review → strength → priority → action → follow-up**

🔎 → ⭐ → 🎯 → 🔧 → 📌

## What evidence do you draw on when writing a report or discussing a student's progress?

### I triangulate evidence so that the report reflects the learner, not a single score.

I draw on formal and informal assessment, classwork, homework, questioning, retrieval, observation, participation and progress over time. I consider patterns rather than isolated performances and distinguish attainment from effort or engagement.

The final judgement should be accurate, evidence-based and consistent with the school's reporting procedures.

**formal → formative → observation → pattern → judgement**

📝 → 🔄 → 👁️ → 🔎 → ⚖️

## How do you communicate a concern about progress while remaining constructive and solution-focused?

### I describe the evidence clearly, protect the student's dignity and move quickly toward support and next steps.

I avoid vague or judgemental language. I explain the specific concern, give examples, acknowledge strengths and describe what has already been tried. I then identify a realistic next step and, where appropriate, agree how progress will be monitored.

The purpose is not simply to report a problem; it is to create a shared plan for improvement.

**evidence → concern → strength → support → review**

📊 → ⚠️ → ⭐ → 🤲 → 🔎

## How do you report relevant progress or concerns within the school?

### I share the right information with the right people through the school's agreed support structures.

If a concern is significant, persistent or relevant to student support, I record objective evidence and communicate through the appropriate channels, such as the year head, tutor, SEN/AEN team, guidance, management or student-support structures as appropriate.

I distinguish between information colleagues need to support the student and information that does not need to be shared, and I follow school policy and professional confidentiality.

**record → communicate → collaborate → support → monitor**

📝 → 🗣️ → 🤝 → 🤲 → 👁️

## How do confidentiality and GDPR affect assessment records and reporting?

### Assessment information should be accurate, necessary, secure and shared only for a legitimate educational purpose.

I use school-approved systems, keep records factual and proportionate, protect access to personal information and avoid discussing student data in inappropriate settings. I share information only with those who need it for their professional role and follow school procedures for storage, retention and disposal.

Confidentiality does not prevent appropriate professional sharing; it means sharing relevant information responsibly and securely.

**accurate → necessary → secure → appropriate → accountable**

✅ → 🎯 → 🔒 → 👥 → 🛡️
