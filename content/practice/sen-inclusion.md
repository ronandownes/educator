## SEN, AEN and inclusion — working distinction

### SEN gives me important identified information; AEN reminds me to notice the wider barriers; inclusion is the outcome I am trying to achieve.

| SEN | AEN | Inclusion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identified special educational needs, Student Support Files, professional reports and agreed accommodations | Broader or contextual needs such as EAL, anxiety, bereavement, interrupted attendance, literacy/numeracy gaps or temporary barriers | Meaningful participation, belonging, access to worthwhile learning, progress and increasing independence |

I use diagnosis and professional information responsibly, but I still observe the individual learner and translate recommendations into subject-specific classroom practice. Where a need is significant but not diagnosed, I respond to the evidence rather than waiting for a label. I coordinate support with the student, family and relevant staff, monitor whether it is helping, and adjust or fade support where appropriate.

**Questions to keep ready:** How do I translate a recommendation into classroom practice? What do I do when a need is significant but not diagnosed? When is in-class support preferable to withdrawal? How do I maintain challenge while widening access? What evidence tells me the support is working?

## What is your approach to SEN / AEN and inclusion?

### Support participation. Build independence. Maintain high expectations.

I start with the **learner**, not the label.

I use the student-support **profile** and collaborate with the AEN team and other staff.

I identify the **barriers**, adapt **access** and provide appropriate **support**.

That support should increase meaningful **participation** and **independence**, rather than create unnecessary dependence.

I maintain appropriate challenge and **ambition** because an additional need does not define a student’s potential.

## Identify the educational needs of your students?

### Gather information, observe carefully, assess learning and collaborate.

I begin with the information legitimately available to me: handover, Student Support Files or plans, previous assessment, accommodations and advice from relevant staff.

I then compare that information with what I observe in class. I use low-stakes assessment, questioning, student work and discussion to establish strengths as well as barriers.

A diagnosis is useful information, but I still need to understand how that particular learner presents in my classroom.

## Why does patience matter in AEN?

### What I see on the surface may not reveal the learning, processing or regulation load a student is carrying.

Patience is not lowering expectations. It means allowing enough safety, processing time and appropriate support for a student to enter or re-enter the learning successfully.

Some students may mask distress, confusion, anxiety or autistic traits effectively. Outward composure therefore does not necessarily mean that the learning environment is easy for them to manage.

I use predictable routines, clear instructions, processing time, calm communication and appropriate regulation supports while maintaining the learning expectation.

## How do you differentiate in a mixed-needs class?

### Same core learning; different routes, supports and levels of challenge.

I vary the entry point, scaffolding, representation, pace, task complexity and degree of independence.

Where possible, students remain part of the same learning conversation. One learner may need a worked example or concrete representation while another moves into application, justification or extension.

Formative assessment tells me who needs more support and who needs more challenge.

## How do you differentiate without creating five different lessons?

### Differentiate the route before differentiating the destination.

I usually retain a common learning intention and vary the support around it: prompts, examples, numbers, visual representation, vocabulary, questioning, pace and extension.

That is manageable for the teacher and keeps students connected to the same classroom learning.

Separate work may sometimes be necessary, but it should arise from a genuine identified need rather than being the automatic meaning of differentiation.

## What is the difference between mixed ability and AEN?

### Attainment and additional need are related sometimes, but they are not the same thing.

Mixed ability refers to variation in prior learning, attainment, confidence, pace and fluency.

AEN refers to additional barriers or needs affecting access, learning, communication, processing, attention, regulation or participation.

A highly able learner may have significant additional needs, while a learner without a diagnosis may require considerable classroom support.

## How do you use a Student Support File or student-support information?

### The file is not the support; it informs the support.

I identify the student's strengths, priority needs, agreed targets, accommodations and strategies that have been found to work.

I translate that into classroom decisions about instructions, seating, questioning, scaffolding, assessment, processing time and check-ins.

I also contribute appropriate evidence from my classroom so that support can be reviewed rather than remaining static.

## How do you know whether an AEN support is working?

### Support should produce evidence of greater access, participation, progress or independence.

I look at student work, engagement, assessment evidence, level of prompting required and the student's ability to transfer learning independently.

I also listen to the student and communicate with relevant colleagues.

If the strategy is not working, I review and adapt rather than continuing it simply because it was written in a plan.

## How do you support an autistic student?

### Clarity, predictability and dignity — without making assumptions about ability.

I use predictable structure, concise instructions, visual information, appropriate processing time and advance warning of significant changes where possible.

I avoid unnecessary public pressure and remain aware of sensory and regulation needs.

I also maintain academic challenge. Autism does not imply low ability, and some autistic learners require extension as much as they require structure.

## How do you support a student with ADHD?

### Make organisation visible and reduce unnecessary competition for attention.

I use predictable routines, concise instructions, visible steps, manageable chunks and clear transitions.

I may use strategic seating, short check-ins, appropriate movement, timers or intermediate goals where helpful.

I also distinguish between deliberate non-compliance and difficulty with attention, inhibition, working memory or organisation while maintaining clear expectations.

## How do you support dyslexia in Maths or Science?

### Remove the literacy barrier without removing the mathematical or scientific demand.

I use clear layout, manageable text, diagrams, worked examples and explicit teaching of vocabulary and command words.

I reduce unnecessary copying and break reading-heavy questions into manageable stages.

I also ask whether an incorrect response reflects a conceptual misunderstanding or difficulty accessing the language of the task.

## How do you support a student with dyscalculia or significant numeracy difficulty?

### Make quantity, number and mathematical structure visible.

I use concrete materials, number lines, visual models and repeated structures before expecting fluent symbolic manipulation.

I break multi-step procedures into manageable parts and revisit important number relationships frequently.

The aim is not merely easier Mathematics; it is better access to the underlying mathematical idea.

## How do you support speech, language and communication needs?

### Reduce unnecessary language load without reducing thinking.

I keep verbal instructions concise, provide key information visually and model the first step.

I explicitly teach vocabulary, allow processing time and check understanding without unnecessarily putting the student on the spot.

In Maths and Science, a student can understand the concept but lose access because the instruction itself is too linguistically demanding.

## How do you reduce cognitive overload?

### Keep the thinking that matters and remove avoidable load around it.

I chunk information, sequence examples carefully, reduce unnecessary copying and verbal overload, make important information visible and connect new learning to prior knowledge.

I model a manageable number of steps before moving towards guided and independent practice.

Reducing cognitive load does not mean making the intellectual content trivial.

## How do you support a student experiencing anxiety or emotional-regulation difficulty?

### Regulation can be the bridge back to learning.

I remain calm, reduce unnecessary public pressure and use clear, predictable communication.

Where appropriate and consistent with the student's plan, I may allow processing or regulation time, use an achievable first step or an agreed quiet-space arrangement.

The aim is not indefinite avoidance of learning. It is helping the student regulate sufficiently to participate again.

## What if behaviour appears to be linked to an additional need?

### Understand the function of the behaviour while maintaining the boundary.

I ask what may be driving the behaviour: inaccessible work, communication difficulty, anxiety, sensory overload, attention, frustration or repeated failure.

That may change how I intervene—for example through a visual prompt, an achievable start, processing time or reduced verbal demand.

Understanding behaviour is not the same as accepting unsafe or persistently disruptive behaviour.

## How do you communicate with a student with additional needs?

### Make communication clear, accessible, respectful and responsive to the individual.

I use concise language, appropriate visual support, sufficient processing time and checks for understanding.

I avoid assuming that silence means lack of understanding or that verbal fluency necessarily means the student has processed everything.

Most importantly, I communicate directly with the student rather than always speaking about them through another adult.

## How do you work effectively with an SNA?

### The teacher leads the learning; the SNA supports access, participation and independence.

I clarify the learning intention and relevant supports so that the adults working with the student are aligned.

I value the SNA's observations and knowledge of the learner while retaining responsibility for teaching, assessment and classroom decisions.

We should avoid creating unnecessary dependence. Effective support gradually enables the student to do more for themselves.

## How do you collaborate with the AEN team, year head and other staff?

### A student experiences one school, not a collection of disconnected classrooms.

I share relevant classroom observations, listen to colleagues who know the student in other contexts and use agreed strategies consistently.

Where a concern spans academic, behavioural, emotional or attendance issues, joined-up communication is particularly important.

Collaboration also helps us identify whether a difficulty is occurring across settings or is specific to one context.

## How do you use a seating plan to support AEN?

### Seating is a learning and access tool, not merely a behaviour tool.

I consider visibility, hearing, sensory needs, attention, peer dynamics, confidence, proximity to support and my own ability to circulate.

I do not assume that every learner with a particular diagnosis needs the same seat.

I review the arrangement when the evidence suggests it is not working.

## How do you make assessment fair and inclusive?

### Keep the learning being assessed central and remove irrelevant barriers where appropriate.

I use clear questions and instructions, appropriate accommodations, accessible presentation and more than one source of evidence where the assessment allows it.

Formative assessment is particularly useful because questioning, observation, mini-whiteboards, discussion and student work can reveal understanding that one written test may not.

Fair does not necessarily mean identical; it means assessing the intended learning appropriately.

I follow agreed accommodations and use assessment evidence to decide whether to reteach, change representation, provide targeted practice or increase challenge.

## How do you plan for students with AEN?

### Plan access before the lesson rather than trying to retrofit support during it.

I use my knowledge of the class profile when planning the learning intention, examples, vocabulary, representations, questioning, grouping and assessment.

I anticipate likely barriers and build in supports that may benefit many learners before adding individual accommodations where necessary.

That allows me to retain a coherent lesson rather than producing a separate lesson for every student.

## How does UDL fit with AEN?

### Design broad access from the beginning, then add targeted support where necessary.

UDL encourages me to anticipate variation rather than wait for students to fail before adapting.

I can provide clear structure, multiple representations, accessible language and appropriate ways of participating from the outset.

Individual students may still need targeted accommodations or interventions. UDL complements individual support; it does not replace it.

## How do you support a reluctant or disengaged learner?

### Begin with access, relationship and achievable success.

I try to determine whether disengagement is linked to confidence, repeated failure, difficulty accessing the work, relationships or something outside the immediate lesson.

I use a manageable first step, private encouragement and purposeful support rather than beginning with public confrontation.

Success can then become the first step back into participation.

## How do you engage a quiet student or one who rarely puts up their hand?

### Participation should not depend on confidence in volunteering publicly.

I use think time, think-pair-share, mini-whiteboards, targeted but supportive questioning and opportunities to rehearse an answer before speaking publicly.

That gives me evidence from quieter students without making classroom participation dependent on the fastest or most confident hands.

## How do you support a highly able student who also has additional needs?

### Additional need and high ability can coexist.

I provide whatever structure, regulation support or accommodation the learner needs while continuing to offer intellectual challenge.

That may include richer problems, generalisation, justification, multiple methods, independent investigation or extension.

Support should never become an unintended ceiling on expectation.

## How do you ensure all students are appropriately supported and challenged?

### Support and challenge are two sides of differentiation.

I use formative assessment to establish where students are and then adjust the amount of structure, complexity, prompting and independence.

Students who need support receive a clearer route into the learning. Students who are secure move towards transfer, justification, unfamiliar applications or deeper reasoning.

Nobody should spend the lesson either lost or waiting.

## How do you maintain high standards while remaining inclusive?

### Support changes the route, not the ambition.

I distinguish between lowering a barrier and lowering an expectation.

A student may need additional processing time, clearer language, a visual representation or greater scaffolding while still working towards meaningful learning.

## What if it becomes clear that a student needs extra help?

### Notice early, identify the barrier, intervene and review.

I first use classroom evidence to establish what the student is finding difficult rather than simply giving more of the same work.

I provide an appropriate targeted intervention and monitor the response.

If the difficulty persists or suggests a wider need, I communicate through the school's support structures so that the response becomes collaborative and evidence-informed.

### Vocabulary Retrieval Chain

## What do you do when an intervention is not working?

### Change the strategy, not the ambition.

I return to the evidence: What exactly is the student still unable to access? Is the barrier what we originally thought it was?

I speak with the learner and relevant colleagues, adjust the intervention and establish what evidence will tell us whether the new approach is working.

Support planning should be iterative rather than fixed.

## How do you involve student voice in AEN support?

### The student should increasingly understand what helps them learn.

I ask students what makes learning easier or harder and compare that with classroom evidence.

Where appropriate, I help them develop language to describe their needs and request reasonable support.

That builds metacognition and self-advocacy and moves support towards independence.

## What does independence mean in AEN?

### The success of support is not measured by how much help we give, but by what the learner can increasingly do.

Initially a student may require significant modelling, prompting or adult support.

I try to fade that support as competence develops—moving from modelled to guided to increasingly independent performance.

Independence also includes knowing when and how to ask appropriately for support.

## How do you support transitions for a student with additional needs?

### Prepare what can be predicted and support what cannot.

For significant changes—new classroom, teacher, routine, task or programme—I give clear information in advance where appropriate.

I use predictable cues, explicit routines and communication with relevant staff.

After the transition, I check how the student is managing rather than assuming that preparation alone solved the difficulty.

## How do you protect dignity when providing additional support?

### Support the student without making the student the spectacle.

Where possible, I build supports into ordinary classroom routines so students are not unnecessarily singled out.

I use private prompts, discreet check-ins and resources that preserve belonging.

I also speak directly and respectfully to the learner and avoid defining them publicly by a diagnosis or difficulty.

## How do you balance withdrawal support with inclusion in the mainstream class?

### Targeted support should strengthen participation, not disconnect the learner from the curriculum.

Withdrawal can be valuable where a learner needs intensive or specific teaching.

I would want clear objectives for that support and communication between the mainstream and support teachers so that learning connects across settings.

The arrangement should be reviewed in terms of its actual benefit to the learner.

## How do you make practical Science inclusive and safe?

### Inclusion widens participation; it never lowers the safety threshold.

I make safety instructions concise and explicit, model procedures, use visual steps and clarify group roles.

I check understanding before equipment is used and circulate actively.

Where a learner requires additional organisation, language or sensory support, I plan that access while maintaining the same essential safety requirements.

## How do you ensure a student with AEN reaches their full potential?

### Start from strengths, remove barriers, monitor progress and keep the next challenge visible.

I combine appropriate support with ambitious but realistic expectations.

I use assessment evidence to ensure the learner is progressing rather than merely completing work.

As access improves, I reduce unnecessary support and increase independence, transfer and challenge.

## What does successful inclusion look like?

### The student belongs to the learning, not merely to the room.

Successful inclusion means meaningful participation, appropriate challenge, progress, relationships and increasing independence.

It does not require everybody to learn in precisely the same way.

It means unnecessary barriers are reduced while the learner remains an authentic member of the class and its learning community.
